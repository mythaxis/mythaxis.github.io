Short Reviews – February to June, 2026

by Andrew Leon Hudson

The customary closer: a set of short reviews that bring together three stories that caught on the editor's mind while reading through the year. This time they're somewhat unbalanced, two very short ones and one considerably longer; the first pair of passing similarity, the latter not remotely. But all interesting, and free to read at the links you'll find waiting.

Short Reviews – February to June, 2026

Andrew Leon Hudson

F irst up, nine issues on, a first proper mention here for Daikaiju Zine, named for and home to strange creatures (kaiju), specifically large strange creatures (daikaiju), though in Jenny Hart’s story size is a relative factor. And strangeness, well, inherently so whatever the context, really. There is no “strange” without an opinion about “normal” against which to frame it.

Creatures, though, no question. There’s humans all over the place. Beneath, Below is not a long story, and I don’t want to spoil, so I’ll focus mostly on the fact that it’s set in an inconceivably distant lunar habitat – not our moon; our habitat only through artefice – and does a fine job of presenting the subtle and not so subtle stressors of life lived in a superficially comfortable extreme. Which is how it will have to be, when we finally do leave the familiar environments of home to explore what the outer universe consists of. We humans, I mean.

S taying satalitical, though at least spacially closer to home, Ashlee Lhamon gives I think an even briefer glimpse of space station life – first in terms of word count, then in terms of nasty, brutish, and short. Moon Drama (spoiler avoided) is also speculative employment fiction, that hill I choose to die on, this time giving a humorous glimpse of the trials and tribulations of taking your career off-planet, though this time it sounds like the kaiju are very much dai (if not die).

Call me “Neppo-Daddy”, but a month or few ago Foofaraw Press named me “Federmeister” (which translates to “the greatest editor in history”, or something, basically that, let’s move on) and now here I am saying “They’re great, go read them!” But since they are great, and because you should read them, there’s really no conflict of interest here and so I don’t really know why I mentioned it (except that I’m proud of the feather in my cap, obviously).

B oth the previous stories came out back in March, but to wrap things up we’ll close in on the present – on the one hand, because The Trident-Tailed Water Monster appears in The Future Fire’s May 2026 issue, and on the other because Rae Zalopany introduces us to a run-down corner of Florida with a kind of timeless, contemporary air.

I say this because in a way the story feels like (if it wasn’t it wasn’t for the appearance of a mobile phone) it could take place any time since say the 70s. Perhaps because the protagonist’s perspective (uncertainty about the intentions of others, uncertainty about those of herself) and the environments she occupies (decaying social spaces, natural zones that don’t feel ours, the liminal borders between them) are in the greater scheme of things persistent conditions, even if the players and the places shift.

I feel myself waffling. It’s the story of Natalie, alone by choice but hardly happy about it. She dreams of something primal and strange; encounters something ancient that offers deepest desires granted; must decide whether it comes at a high cost or is in trade for something she doesn’t value at all. Also sees and thinks about the collapsing mundanity of the world we’re all living in. If you can call it that, what we’re doing.

Andrew Leon Hudson

Andrew is a technical writer by day, and is technically a writer by night as well. In addition to editing Mythaxis he has been published in a small handful of quality zines, and co-authored a serialised alternate history adventure novel. He lives in Barcelona, Spain, and doesn’t do things online often enough to count.
